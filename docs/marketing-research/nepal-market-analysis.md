# Nepal market analysis — website-development positioning for Yugya Technologies

**Prepared for:** Yugya Technologies (`https://www.yugya.com/`)  
**Date:** 17 Nov 2026  
**Scope:** Evidence-based market and customer-positioning research for website-development services in Nepal. This document does **not** change any `app/` or `components/` file.  
**Primary question:** Given how people in Nepal find, judge and buy online services, what should Yugya's website-development positioning say, and what should it avoid saying?

---

## 0. How to read this report

### Evidence labels

Every substantive claim carries one of these labels:

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Evidence-backed finding** | A fact reported by a regulator, central bank, established news organisation, statistical compilation or a directly observed live page. Secondary data are named as such. A finding is still limited by its source's method. |
| **Third-party opinion** | A claim, recommendation, anecdote or price range published by a vendor, practitioner or industry body. It shows how a supplier frames the market; it is **not** verified customer evidence. |
| **Professional recommendation** | An analyst conclusion for Yugya, drawn from the evidence and from Yugya's existing truth-in-description positioning. It is a recommendation, not a measured result. |
| **HYPOTHESIS** | A plausible customer belief, trigger, expectation or segment behaviour that was **not** established by the research. It must be tested before being stated as fact in public copy. |
| **ASSUMPTION** | A working definition used to organise the research; it is not a claim that the segment has been measured. |

### Method

- The built-in web-search integration returned `HTTP 402`, so DuckDuckGo HTML result pages were used for discovery. Underlying pages were then opened and read directly; no fact in this report rests on a search snippet alone.
- One PDF — the Hotel Association Nepal January 2025 newsletter — was downloaded and its text extracted locally.
- Publication dates are recorded where the page shows them. Where no date is displayed, this is stated explicitly.
- All web sources were accessed on **17 Nov 2026**.
- This is **not** a survey, an interview programme, a representative sample of Nepali business owners, or a lead-forecasting model. No customer quotes, survey results, conversion rates, satisfaction figures or market shares were invented.

### Important data cautions

1. **Ad reach is not the same as active users.** DataReportal's Facebook figure is an advertising-audience estimate, not a count of people who use Facebook daily [1].
2. **Connections and subscriptions are not people.** Nepal's mobile-connection and broadband-subscription figures exceed the population partly because people hold multiple SIMs or connections [1][3].
3. **Wallet users and QR transactions are not unique shoppers.** The Nepal Rastra Bank figures show transaction scale and adoption, not a count of distinct businesses or consumers [4].
4. **Vendor price bands are not market rates.** Published website-price guides show how suppliers package and explain work; they are not verified benchmarks and are not Yugya prices [11][12].
5. **A platform's self-description is not proof of customer preference.** A vendor saying "businesses need websites" is third-party opinion, not customer evidence [11][16][17].

## 1. Executive summary

1. **Nepal is mobile-first and internet-active, but not universally connected.** DataReportal's late-2025 compilation reports 32.4 million mobile connections, 16.6 million internet users (56.0% of the population) and 13.0 million people offline (44.0%) [1]. NTA data reported in mid-2026 show very high broadband-subscription penetration but also multi-SIM inflation [3]. **Professional recommendation:** write for a phone screen first and do not treat "online" as universal.

2. **Social media is the discovery layer, not a stable substitute for an owned site.** Facebook remains the largest reported social platform by ad reach in Nepal, while Messenger, Instagram and LinkedIn also have meaningful reach [1]. At the same time, the 2025 social-media restriction drive showed how access to major platforms can change abruptly [6], and 2026 registration and e-commerce rules now cover online sellers and digital platforms [7][8]. **Professional recommendation:** position a website as the business-owned reference point that works *with* social media, not as a replacement for it.

3. **Trust is a commercial problem in Nepal, not merely a design concern.** Reporting on Nepal Police Cyber Bureau data found hundreds of online-store fraud cases and fake-social-account cases in the cited calendar year [9]. A 2026 legal-practitioner guide advises businesses to secure official handles, preserve evidence and link official channels from their websites when impersonation occurs [10]. New registration and complaint requirements reinforce the move toward identifiable online sellers [7][8]. **Professional recommendation:** make official identity, contact details, scope, policies and handover visible; never borrow customer trust from unverifiable claims.

4. **Digital payment adoption makes a real website more useful for commerce.** Nepal Rastra Bank data reported in June 2026 show wallet users rising from 6.3 million to 28.3 million and monthly QR transactions from fewer than 200,000 to 59.3 million between mid-August 2020 and mid-May 2026; QR use was described as common in shops, restaurants, supermarkets and small businesses [4]. **Professional recommendation:** where e-commerce is genuinely in scope, discuss local payment methods and clear payment instructions — but do not imply every site needs a full checkout.

5. **The strongest observed buyer questions are commercial and practical, not technical.** Vendor FAQs, guides and competitor pages repeatedly surface cost, annual running costs, timelines, ownership, mobile behaviour, Google visibility, support and handover [11][12][17]. These are **published supplier observations**, not direct customer quotes. **Professional recommendation:** answer those questions early in plain language; keep technical detail as supporting evidence.

6. **Six useful segments emerge, but their internal motivations remain hypotheses.** Hospitality has the clearest independent sector scale evidence [13]. Social sellers and shops are supported by payment, fraud, registration and social-commerce evidence [4][7][9][16][17]. Education, NGOs, professional services and operationally complex SMEs are plausible targets with thinner direct customer evidence; their needs must be validated. **Professional recommendation:** use segment cues in copy only when the page is genuinely relevant to that reader — for example, a restaurant example on a restaurant-oriented section — not as invented audience statistics.

7. **Yugya's defensible position is specific, not loud.** Competitor and language research already in the repository shows a market crowded with "best", "No.1", large self-reported counts and keyword-heavy headlines. **Professional recommendation:** compete on plain explanations, visible boundaries, real named work, client-owned access, written scope and honest limits. Do not adopt vendor price ranges, guaranteed rankings, invented testimonials or unattributed performance claims.

## 2. Market conditions

### 2.1 Connectivity: large reach, unequal access

**Evidence-backed finding.** The DataReportal *Digital 2026: Nepal* compilation, published 8 Nov 2025 and reflecting late-2025 data, reports:

- 32.4 million mobile connections, equivalent to 109% of the population [1].
- 16.6 million internet users, or 56.0% of the population [1].
- 13.0 million people offline, or 44.0% of the population [1].
- 77.0% of the population living in rural areas and a median age of 25.3 [1].
- A reported median fixed-internet download speed of 79.79 Mbps [1].

**Evidence-backed finding.** NTA figures reported by *NepalNews* in June 2026 cover 30,986,524 broadband subscriptions in the mid-February-to-mid-May 2026 period: 27,498,552 mobile broadband and 3,456,331 fixed wired subscriptions. The reported 146.55% penetration is inflated by multiple subscriptions per person; fixed wired connections grew more quickly in the cited period [3].

**Evidence-backed finding.** DataReportal's *Digital 2025: Nepal* figures are lower for mobile connections and social identities than the 2026 edition, but the publisher revised underlying series between editions. Naive year-on-year comparison would be misleading [1][2].

**Professional recommendation.** Build for mobile screens and variable network conditions. Avoid copy that assumes every customer is constantly online or has a fixed broadband connection. A site should be a durable place customers can return to when social feeds are unavailable, but it should not be described as reaching the 44% who are offline.

### 2.2 Discovery: search plus social, with platform volatility

**Evidence-backed finding.** DataReportal's late-2025 figures report 14.8 million social-media identities (50.0% of the population, or 75.6% of adults) and platform advertising reach of 14.8 million for Facebook, 11.0 million for Messenger, 4.35 million for Instagram, 2.30 million for LinkedIn and 342,000 for X [1]. LinkedIn's reported ad reach grew 21.1% in the cited period [1].

**Third-party opinion / secondary compilation.** A Nepali digital-marketing practitioner's 2026 statistics compilation, citing StatCounter's August 2026 data, assigns Google 96.33% of the measured search-engine market and Facebook 88.69% of measured social referrals; it also reports 57.13% mobile and 42.42% desktop traffic. The author sells digital-marketing services and explicitly notes that DataReportal does not publish YouTube or TikTok reach for Nepal. These are useful directional observations, not independently audited demand figures or Yugya traffic forecasts [5].

**Evidence-backed finding.** In September 2025, the Kathmandu Post explained the government's Social Media Directives 2080 registration drive. Facebook, Messenger, Instagram, X, LinkedIn and YouTube were among the restricted platforms at that time, while registered services such as TikTok and Viber remained accessible. The article also described halted creator earnings and confusion over services such as WhatsApp and Gmail [6].

**Professional recommendation.** Treat social platforms as discovery and conversation channels, and a website as the stable record of what the business actually offers. Do not sell a website as "the only channel that matters" or as a guaranteed source of customers. A more defensible line is: social helps people find you; your own site gives them one place to check details.

### 2.3 Trust, impersonation and regulation

**Evidence-backed finding.** The Kathmandu Post's February 2024 report on online-shopping fraud cited Nepal Police Cyber Bureau figures: 4,646 cyber-crime cases in the previous fiscal year, including 460 online-store fraud cases and 37 fake-social-media-account cases in the cited calendar year [9]. The article describes fake social accounts taking advance payments and then disappearing [9]. These are reported cases, not an estimate of all fraud.

**Evidence-backed finding.** A Kathmandu Post report on the 2025 social-media restrictions shows that platform availability and creator monetisation can change through regulation [6].

**Evidence-backed finding.** In August 2026, Fiscal Nepal reported that online business registration had been made mandatory for Facebook, TikTok and Instagram sellers, websites, apps and digital-service providers, with legal action threatened under the Electronic Commerce Act 2081. The report links the move to complaints about online fraud and urges consumers to use registered platforms [7].

**Evidence-backed finding.** The Business and Human Rights Centre, republishing a Kathmandu Post report in January 2026, describes the E-commerce Guidelines 2026 under the E-commerce Act 2025. The guidelines are reported to cover platforms including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and WhatsApp and to address registration, audits or certification, complaint mechanisms and local representation for foreign platforms. The report notes that much growth came from unregistered social sellers [8].

**Third-party opinion.** Axcel Law Associates' March 2026 guide advises Nepali businesses to secure official handles, preserve evidence of impersonation, use platform complaint routes and escalate fraud to the Nepal Police Cyber Bureau. It specifically recommends linking official channels from a business website and treating social-account protection as part of brand protection [10]. This is legal-practitioner advice, not a customer study.

**Professional recommendation.** Trust copy should be concrete: show real named projects and live URLs, identify the people doing the work, state what is included, publish contact routes and explain ownership. Avoid "trusted by hundreds", "100% secure", "guaranteed results" or any claim that cannot be independently checked.

### 2.4 Payments and transactions

**Evidence-backed finding.** People's Review, citing Nepal Rastra Bank data for mid-August 2020 to mid-May 2026, reports that digital-wallet users rose from 6.3 million to 28.3 million, monthly QR transactions grew from fewer than 200,000 to 59.3 million, and mobile-banking users exceeded 30 million. QR payments were described as common in shops, restaurants, supermarkets and small businesses. Digital literacy and cybersecurity were named as continuing challenges [4].

**Professional recommendation.** For commerce projects, discuss how customers will actually pay and how confirmation will reach them. Do not assume a card-first checkout. Equally, do not turn every brochure site into an e-commerce project: a restaurant that needs a phone-friendly menu has a different scope from a shop that needs catalogue, stock, payment and delivery records.

## 4. Website objections to expect — and how to answer them honestly

The wording below is a **message hypothesis**, not a transcript of customer speech. The basis column states whether the objection is supported by evidence, vendor opinion or remains untested.

| Objection to test | Basis | Honest response direction | Do not say |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "My Facebook or Instagram page is enough; that is where customers find me." | Social reach is real [1]. **HYPOTHESIS:** owners may treat it as sufficient. A vendor article asserts that most local shop owners are found via Facebook, but gives no method; treat that assertion as **third-party opinion**, not a survey [11]. | "Keep the channels that bring you enquiries. Add one official place where people can check what you offer, your hours, contact details and policies." | "Facebook is dead", "social media is rented land" presented as fact, or any claim that a website will replace existing enquiries. |
| "I cannot afford a website." | Vendor pricing guides publish wide ranges and list recurring domain, hosting and maintenance costs [11][12]. **Third-party opinion** about bands; **HYPOTHESIS** that price is the first objection. | "The price depends on pages and features. | "Will it work on people's phones?" | Mobile connections exceed the population and practitioner data report a mobile-majority traffic split [1][5]. **Evidence-backed** importance of mobile; **HYPOTHESIS** that owners ask this exact question. | "We check the site on phones, tablets and computers before handover." | "Who will update it after launch?" | Vendor pricing guides list maintenance as a recurring category [12]; competitor research in the repository shows handover and maintenance are rarely explained clearly. **Third-party opinion / internal observation**; **HYPOTHESIS** about the speaker. | State who updates content, what support window exists and what is scoped separately. Keep Yugya's verified 30-day bug-fix window and 1–2 business day response aim exact if used. | "Unlimited support", "always available", "24/7", or "we handle everything" unless true. |
| "How do I know it is a real business online?" | Online-store fraud, fake accounts and registration drives make identity verification salient [7][9][10]. **Evidence-backed** problem; **HYPOTHESIS** that it is a website-purchase objection. | Show real contact routes, official links, named people and verifiable work. Present the site as one trust signal among several. | "This site proves we are legitimate" or a guarantee that customers will trust the business. |
| "A site will not help because customers use TikTok/Instagram/WhatsApp." | Social channels are demonstrably important [1][16][17]. **HYPOTHESIS** about the objection. | "Those channels still matter. The site is where you keep the details you control and where official links can point." | Dismissing social platforms or implying younger customers do not use them. |

**Professional recommendation.** The common thread is not a demand for more technology. It is a demand for certainty about money, ownership, effort, visibility and support. Those are the themes the copy should answer before listing implementation details.

## 6. Six customer segments

The six segments below are working definitions (**ASSUMPTION**), chosen because each has a plausible website job, a distinct trigger and a distinct post-purchase expectation. Unless explicitly labelled **Evidence-backed finding**, the motivations, objections and expectations are **HYPOTHESIS**.

### Segment 1 — Hospitality: restaurants, cafés, hotels and homestays

**Evidence-backed finding.** Hotel Association Nepal's January 2025 newsletter, citing the National Statistics Office's National Hotel and Restaurant Survey, reports 142,223 accommodation and restaurant establishments, including 126,859 restaurants, 11,919 non-star hotels and lodges, and 1,345 homestays. Bagmati alone accounts for 52,123 establishments [13].

**Evidence-backed channel finding.** Foodmandu's own restaurant-listing page claims access to more than 500,000 customers and names Kathmandu, Bhaktapur, Chitwan and Butwal [14]. This is the platform's claim, not an independent measurement.

**Third-party opinion.** Vendor guides describe digital menus, photos, contact details and online ordering as common hospitality needs; the competitor research already in the repository observes the same pattern. These are supplier observations, not customer quotes [11][16].

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** A guest searches for a menu, location, room type or opening hours before visiting; the owner notices repeated phone questions or wants a stable place to link from delivery and social profiles.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Show current menu or services, photos, location, contact routes and hours in a way that works on a phone. For hotels, show rooms and amenities; for restaurants, make the menu easy to reopen and share.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** Staff can update prices, hours and seasonal items without a developer for every small change; the site works on phones used by guests; contact and map details are correct; social and delivery profiles link to the official site.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Use restaurant and hotel cues only where relevant: "menu customers can open on a phone", "rooms, location and contact in one place", "a clear link target from your delivery and social profiles". Point to the real Kohinoor project only where provenance and attribution are accurate; do not turn a live client site into an unearned performance claim.

**Validate with.** Ask 15–20 owners across restaurant, café, hotel and homestay types what customers ask before visiting, who updates the menu, which platform drives enquiries and what broke the last time information changed.

### Segment 2 — Local retail and social sellers

**ASSUMPTION.** This segment includes shops, boutiques, beauty and personal-service businesses, and Facebook or Instagram sellers handling product enquiries by chat.

**Evidence-backed finding.** Wallet users, QR transactions and mobile banking have expanded substantially, with QR payments reported as common in shops, restaurants, supermarkets and small businesses [4]. Online-store fraud cases and fake social accounts have also been reported [9]. Registration rules now cover major selling platforms and websites [7][8].

**Third-party opinion.** Social-commerce guides from KODEEFY and Kokil Thapa describe discovery on Facebook and Instagram, payment links or wallets, and the operational limits of using chat as the only order record [16][17]. These guides are written by platform vendors/practitioners; anecdotes about client results are not verified customer evidence.

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** Messages overlap, stock or prices become inconsistent, customers ask the same questions repeatedly, or the owner wants a place that looks more permanent than a social profile.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Show product information, price or price range where appropriate, delivery expectations, payment instructions and contact routes. Only some businesses need a full catalogue and checkout.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** Clear product pages customers can reopen, uncomplicated payment confirmation, delivery rules stated before purchase, and a support path that does not depend on one employee's phone.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Avoid a universal "online store" pitch. Separate a simple product/website from a full e-commerce build. Use words such as "catalogue", "checkout", "stock" and "delivery" only when those features are in scope. Do not promise that a website will reduce fraud or increase sales.

**Validate with.** Interview owners who currently sell through Facebook, Instagram or chat. Ask them to reconstruct their last ten enquiries: where they came from, what information was missing, how payment was confirmed and where the order record ended up.

### Segment 3 — Education: schools, colleges and training providers

**ASSUMPTION.** This segment includes private schools, colleges, training centres and preschools that need to inform parents and students.

**Evidence-backed context.** Nepal's internet-user base and social reach are substantial [1], but no source gathered here measures school-website purchasing behaviour. The segment is included because it is an observable supplier category and because Yugya's own project portfolio and language research already identify school and education-related site needs; those internal materials are positioning context, not market evidence.

**Third-party opinion.** Practitioner pages in the wider supplier market publish school-management and school-website service pages; competitor research in the repository also records named school projects. This confirms that suppliers target the segment; it does not establish what parents or administrators value.

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** Admissions season, a new programme, repeated parent questions, or a need to publish notices and documents in one place.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Make admissions information, programmes, contact details, notices and key documents easy to find. For some schools, bilingual display may matter; for others, low-bandwidth access may matter. Both require validation.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** A designated staff member can publish notices; information is readable on parents' phones; contact routes work; the site does not become a second, abandoned noticeboard.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Use education cues only on pages or examples that genuinely address them. Write about "admissions information, programmes, notices and contact details" rather than "digital learning ecosystems". If bilingual delivery is offered, state exactly which languages and pages are included.

**Validate with.** Interview administrators and parents separately. Ask what information parents currently call or message about, who publishes notices, and whether the existing social page or website is checked first.

### Segment 4 — NGOs, INGOs, foundations and social enterprises

**ASSUMPTION.** This segment includes organisations whose credibility depends on visible governance, reporting and programme information.

**Third-party opinion.** WebApp Nepal's NGO/INGO service page frames websites around donor digital due diligence, registration details, annual reports, impact information, bilingual Nepali/English content and low-bandwidth access for rural audiences [15]. The page includes promotional testimonials; those are not verified customer quotes and must not be reused as evidence [15].

**Evidence-backed context.** Nepal's 2026 e-commerce and registration reporting shows a broader move toward formal online accountability [7][8], but it does not prove donor behaviour toward NGO websites.

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** A donor or partner asks for verifiable documents, an annual report cycle begins, or the organisation needs one authoritative place for programme and contact information.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Show who the organisation is, what it does, where it works, how it is registered where relevant, and what reports are available. Language and bandwidth needs depend on the audience.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** Documents can be updated without rebuilding pages; the site is accessible on modest connections; information is consistent across the site and social channels; access is retained by the organisation.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Use transparency language, not uplift language. Say what the site holds and who updates it. Do not claim donor outcomes, publish impact numbers from vendor anecdotes, or promise that a site satisfies registration or reporting rules.

**Validate with.** Speak to programme and communications leads, plus one donor-facing reviewer where possible. Ask which documents are requested, what language audiences use and how field staff access the site.

### Segment 5 — Professional services, clinics and public-facing experts

**ASSUMPTION.** This segment includes consultants, lawyers, accountants, architects, trainers, therapists, clinics and other people whose name and reputation are the product.

**Third-party opinion.** Axcel Law Associates' 2026 guide explicitly names clinics, schools, restaurants, e-commerce brands and public-facing professionals as vulnerable to fake pages and impersonation, and recommends linking official channels from a website [10]. This is legal-practitioner advice, not a survey of those professions.

**Evidence-backed finding.** The Kathmandu Post's fraud reporting documents fake accounts and online-store fraud cases [9]. The 2025 social-media restriction article shows platform-level disruption [6]. Neither establishes how many professionals bought websites as a result.

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** A fake profile appears, a client asks which channel is official, or the professional needs a stable place for services, credentials, contact details and published work.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Present who the person or practice is, what they do, where they work, how to contact them and which profiles are official. Credentials must be checkable.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** The owner can add articles, services or announcements; contact details are consistent; official social links are easy to find; the site loads quickly on mobile.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Favour named people, precise scope and direct contact. Do not invent testimonials, certifications, client logos or "trusted expert" claims. Impersonation copy should say "one official reference point", not "protection from fraud".

**Validate with.** Ask professionals how clients currently verify them, whether they have encountered fake profiles, what they publish most often and who maintains the site.

### Segment 6 — Operationally complex SMEs and automation buyers

**ASSUMPTION.** This segment includes businesses with bookings, inventory, staff workflows, multiple locations, repeated paperwork or customer records — for example, clinics, training providers, hotels, distributors and service firms.

**Evidence-backed context.** Digital-payment growth [4], registration changes [7][8] and the platform-dependency concerns described in practitioner guides [16][17] create conditions where better internal records can matter. No source gathered here directly measures SME automation purchase behaviour in Nepal.

**Third-party opinion.** Kokil Thapa's 2026 guide argues that social campaigns need backend integrations, payment verification and tracking to connect activity to revenue [17]. KODEEFY's guide describes a website as the place for catalogue, checkout, delivery and order history [16]. Both authors sell related services; treat their claims as professional viewpoints.

**HYPOTHESIS — trigger.** Manual work becomes slow or error-prone: duplicate bookings, unclear stock, repeated data entry, lost payment confirmations or staff using incompatible spreadsheets.

**HYPOTHESIS — job to be done.** Replace one repeated, well-understood step with a reliable tool; keep records in one place; show the owner what the tool does and does not do.

**HYPOTHESIS — post-purchase expectations.** Clear user roles, documented access, predictable behaviour, a named support route and an explanation of what happens if a payment or form fails. Buyers may also expect training for staff, but this was not verified.

**Copy implication — Professional recommendation.** Describe automation in everyday terms first: "a small tool that handles a repeated job". Then state the exact job, inputs, outputs and limits. Never imply AI judgement, full automation or integration with a system that is out of scope.

**Validate with.** Map one workflow with 8–12 business owners. Count hand-offs, repeated entries and failure points. Ask what they would trust a tool to do without human checking.

### Segment priority — Professional recommendation

1. **Hospitality and local retail/social sellers** have the strongest evidence for immediate relevance: sector scale, payment adoption and platform-risk evidence [4][9][13][14].
2. **Professional services** have a narrower but concrete trust-related trigger in impersonation guidance [10].
3. **Education, NGOs and operationally complex SMEs** are plausible and strategically attractive, but their needs should be validated before they drive prominent page copy.

This ranking is a judgement about evidence strength, not a claim about total market size or profitability.

## 7. Post-purchase expectations

**Evidence-backed finding.** Published website-price guides list domain, hosting and maintenance as recurring cost categories and warn about hosting lock-in and missing credentials [12]. E-commerce guidelines add complaint mechanisms, registration and oversight expectations to online selling [8]. These sources show what the market discusses; they do not tell us what every Yugya client expects.

**Third-party opinion.** Vendor maintenance and social-commerce guides emphasise updates, backups, order handling, payment confirmation and support response [12][16][17]. These are supplier framings.

**HYPOTHESIS — the following expectations should be tested directly with customers:**

| After purchase, the customer may expect… | Why it matters | What copy can honestly say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A site that works properly on a phone | Mobile connections exceed population and practitioner data show a mobile-majority split [1][5]. | "Checked on phones, tablets and computers before handover." |
| Clear ownership of domain, files, hosting and server access | Vendor guidance flags lock-in and missing credentials as red flags [12]. | State the verified handover: what the client receives and which accounts are in their name. |
| Knowledge of recurring costs | Domain, hosting and maintenance recur in published guides [12]. | Separate one-time build scope from likely recurring items; do not publish vendor figures as Yugya prices. |
| The ability to update simple information | Vendor guides treat maintenance as a distinct service [12]. | State who changes content, what is included in the support window and what is scoped separately. |
| A stated support window | Competitor research in the repository shows after-delivery terms are rarely explained. | Use Yugya's verified 30-day bug-fix window and 1–2 business day response aim exactly where appropriate. |
| Correct official identity and contact information | Fraud, fake pages and registration requirements make verification salient [7][9][10]. | "One official place to check what you offer and how to contact you." Do not promise fraud prevention. |
| Payment clarity where commerce is in scope | Wallet and QR adoption is high, while digital-literacy and cybersecurity challenges remain [4]. | Name supported payment paths only when implemented; explain confirmation and boundaries. |
| Honest reporting | Practitioner guidance criticises vanity metrics and advocates revenue-linked tracking [17]. | Say what the site can show — enquiries, form submissions, page visits if configured — and what it cannot prove. |
| No surprises about what is excluded | Vendor ranges rarely explain boundaries [12]. | Keep the boundaries table prominent: included, scoped separately, out of scope. |

**Professional recommendation.** Post-purchase confidence is built before purchase. Put ownership, recurring costs, support boundaries and handover where a first-time buyer can find them, not only in a deeply buried FAQ.

## 8. Copy implications for Yugya

### 8.1 Positioning statement

**Professional recommendation.** Use a factual, channel-aware position:

> Yugya builds websites for businesses in Nepal. Social platforms help people discover you; your own site gives them one clear place to check what you offer, how to contact you and what you own. We agree scope first, send a written quote, and hand over access to the client.

Before publication, verify every operational clause against Yugya's current approved policies. Do not add this sentence to `app/` or `components/` without an owner decision.

### 8.2 Lead with the customer's job, then the technical detail

| Instead of | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| "Digital ecosystems for modern businesses" | "A website for your business in Nepal." |
| "SEO-friendly responsive architecture" | "A site that works on phones and gives search engines real pages to find." |
| "Custom web development solutions" | "The pages and features we will build, agreed in writing before work starts." |
| "End-to-end technical execution" | "You get the files, settings and access at handover." |
| "Ongoing maintenance packages" | "Regular checks, updates and small fixes, with the exact scope stated." |
| "Automation and AI integration" | "A small tool for one repeated job, with a person checking the result where needed." |

### 8.3 Surface the five commercial answers early

**Professional recommendation.** Every service page or enquiry path should make these answers easy to find:

1. **What will I get?** Pages, features and deliverables in ordinary words.
2. **What will it cost, and what will keep costing?** A written quote after scope; recurring domain, hosting/server and optional maintenance items named without borrowed price figures.
3. **What do I need to provide?** Text, photos, prices, documents, contact details and approvals.
4. **What do I own?** Domain, files, accounts, settings and server access — stated with exact boundaries.
5. **What happens after launch?** Support window, response aim, update responsibility and separate-scope work.

These themes are supported by published supplier questions and guides [11][12][17] and are consistent with Yugya's existing truth-in-description stance. They are not presented as results.

### 8.4 Proof assets to keep visible

**Professional recommendation.**

- Real named projects with live URLs and honest attribution.
- Founder names and portraits where already approved.
- Written scope, milestone billing, boundaries and handover.
- labelled demos and sample data. Do not present demo numbers as customer outcomes.
- Technical detail after the plain explanation, not before it.
- A calm statement of location and working hours where verified.

Do **not** add testimonials, logos, awards, years-of-experience counts, uptime figures, "projects delivered" counts, "trusted by" lines or performance percentages without signed, checkable evidence and owner approval.

### 8.5 Language and terminology

**Professional recommendation.** Keep the public site in plain English, as existing repository language research recommends, but do not make non-technical readers translate technical vocabulary. Explain a term once, near its first use:

- **domain** → your web address;
- **hosting/server** → the computer where the site lives;
- **DNS** → settings that point the address to the right place;
- **responsive** → works on phones, tablets and computers;
- **SEO** → helping the right people find the site when they search;
- **maintenance** → regular checks, updates and small fixes;
- **handover** → files, access and documents given to the client.

Do not machine-translate the site or add romanised Nepali landing pages based on this report. Search-language decisions need Yugya's own Search Console and enquiry data.

### 8.6 Claims to avoid

| Claim type | Why avoid it |
| --- | --- |
| "Best / No.1 / leading website company in Nepal" | Superlatives are common in the supplier market and carry no evidence here. |
| "Trusted by 100+ businesses" | No verified count exists; the repository's competitor research shows how often such counts are self-reported or inconsistent. |
| "Get found on Google" as an unqualified promise | Practitioner data show search matters [5], but no source supports guaranteed visibility or enquiries. |
| "Facebook is rented land" as a fact | It is a vendor metaphor [11], not a measured customer outcome. |
| "We handle everything after launch" | Conflicts with honest boundaries unless explicitly scoped and true. |
| "100% secure / fraud-proof site" | Fake pages and fraud exist [9][10]; no website can be described this way. |
| Published competitor price ranges as "the Nepal market rate" | They are vendor estimates, not verified benchmarks [11][12]. |

### 8.7 Calls to action

**Professional recommendation.** Use low-friction, action-named CTAs:

- "Send an enquiry"
- "Discuss a website"
- "Ask what is included"
- "See live work"
- "Request a written quote"

Avoid "Learn More", "Click Here", "Transform your digital presence" and "Book a free strategy call" unless the actual process matches the words. For a non-technical visitor, "Tell us what customers need to see" is a more useful invitation than "Share your technical requirements."

## 9. Validation plan

Nothing in this report should be promoted from **HYPOTHESIS** to fact without primary evidence. The following methods are recommendations, not completed research.

| Method | How to do it | What it validates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segment interviews | Interview 15–20 decision-makers in each of the six segments. Recruit through existing clients, past enquiries, referrals and social DMs. Use a common guide but sector-specific examples. Record frequencies, not just memorable stories. | Whether objections, triggers and post-purchase expectations recur independently. |
| Lost-lead survey | Send three questions to people who enquired but did not proceed: what they needed, what stopped them, and who they chose instead. Keep responses anonymous where possible. | Real decision blockers and competitor alternatives. |
| Enquiry tagging | Tag every enquiry by segment, source channel, stated trigger and reason for not proceeding. Review monthly. | Which segments and triggers actually generate Yugya opportunities. |
| Search Console and analytics review | Examine Nepal queries, device split, landing pages and form starts by page. Keep English, Devanagari and romanised queries separate. | Whether observed supplier language matches Yugya's real search demand. |
| Message testing | Test two or three plain-language versions of the ownership, cost-boundary and support messages with owners from the same segment. Ask them to explain the message back. | Comprehension, not just preference. Do not publish untested copy as customer language. |
| Quote-page review | Show the written quote and boundaries table to five recent buyers or prospective buyers. Ask what they think is included, what recurs and who owns access. | Whether the proposed "five commercial answers" are actually understood. |
| Post-launch support review | Review support requests for the first 30 days after several projects. Group them by content update, access, bug, question, or new scope. | What "after purchase" really involves. |
| Competitor monitoring | Quarterly, record headline claims, published prices, boundaries and support terms from the same competitor set. | Whether the market's positioning gaps persist. |
| Quote attribution | Ask permission before publishing any customer sentence, logo, screenshot or result. Store the permission and date. | Which proof can ethically be used. |
| Regulatory check | Ask a qualified Nepali legal or compliance professional to interpret registration and e-commerce requirements before any compliance claim is published. | Legal accuracy; Yugya should not give legal advice from marketing copy. |

**Evidence threshold — Professional recommendation.** Treat an insight as validated only when it appears independently across several interviews or enquiry records and can be stated without embellishment. A single vendor anecdote, testimonial or memorable customer phrase is not enough.

## 12. Final position — Professional recommendation

Yugya should not sell a website as a magical growth channel. The evidence supports a narrower, stronger claim: in a market where discovery is social, access is mobile, payments are digitising, platform rules can shift and online identity is under formal scrutiny, a well-built website gives a business a stable, checkable place of its own.

The copy should make that concrete: what the visitor gets, what it costs to run, what the client owns, what the client provides, what happens after launch and what is outside scope. It should use real projects and named people as proof. It should avoid superlatives, borrowed price lists, invented testimonials, guaranteed rankings and any implication that a website alone solves trust, fraud or compliance.

That position is credible in Nepal because it matches both the observed market conditions and Yugya's existing standard of truth in technical descriptions.
